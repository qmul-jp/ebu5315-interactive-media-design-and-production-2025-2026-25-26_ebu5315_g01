Basic

The following 8 theorems belong to the basic theorems

Basic Theorem 1

Angle at the Centre

The angle subtended at the centre of a circle is twice the angle subtended at any point on the circumference, from the same arc.

If two chords from the same arc meet at the centre and at the rim, the “central” angle is the larger one. This links inscribed angles to the geometry of arcs and is a foundation for many circle proofs.

! Exam tip: Check that both angles really stand on the same arc; mixing arcs is the usual trap.
Basic Theorem 2

Angles in a Semicircle

Any angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle (90°).

The diameter is the chord “across the flat side” of the semicircle. Thales’ theorem is the special case of the angle-at-centre rule when the central angle is 180°.

! Quick check: If you can draw a diameter as one side of the triangle, the opposite corner touches the circle at a right angle.
Basic Theorem 3

Cyclic Quadrilateral

Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180°.

A cyclic quadrilateral has all four vertices on one circle. Pairs of opposite angles are supplementary, which often unlocks angle-chasing in harder diagrams.

! Converse idea: If a pair of opposite angles add to 180°, the four points may lie on a circle—useful in “prove concyclic” questions.
Basic Theorem 4

Radius to a Tangent

A radius drawn to the point of contact is perpendicular to the tangent: the angle between them is 90°.

Tangents “kiss” the circle once. The radius aims straight at that touching point, so it meets the tangent line at a right angle—key for coordinate geometry and construction problems.

! Pair with: Equal tangent lengths from an external point—often the same diagram uses both facts.
Basic Theorem 5

Angles in the Same Segment

Angles subtended by the same chord (or arc) at the circumference, on the same side of the chord, are equal.

Think of the chord as a “string” cutting the circle: any viewpoint on the same arc sees the same opening angle. Switching segments flips to the supplementary angle pair.

! Label carefully: “Same segment” means same side of the chord—don’t average angles from opposite arcs.
Basic Theorem 6

Tangents from a Point to a Circle

From an external point, the two tangent segments to a circle have equal lengths.

The two tangents form a symmetric “V” with the point outside the circle. Equal lengths often simplify perimeter or power-of-a-point style reasoning.

! Construction: Right angles appear where each radius meets its tangent—combine with Pythagoras if lengths are given.
Basic Theorem 7

Tangents from a Point (II)

The line from the external point to the centre bisects the angle between the two tangents, and bisects the angle between the two radii to the contact points.

Symmetry about the line through the external point and the centre pairs up equal angles and equal distances—useful in locus and proof questions.

! Visual cue: Mirror symmetry across the “centre line” is the fastest way to remember which angles match.
Basic Theorem 8

Alternate Segment Theorem

The angle between a tangent and a chord through the point of contact equals the angle in the alternate segment (the angle subtended by the chord in the opposite arc).

It connects “tangent direction” to “angles sitting in the other part of the circle.” Often appears in tangent–chord angle-chasing.

! Which segment? The chord splits the circle into two arcs; the alternate segment is the one not containing the angle you first drew with the tangent.
