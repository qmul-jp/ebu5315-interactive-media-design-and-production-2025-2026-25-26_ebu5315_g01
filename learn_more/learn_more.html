Basic

The following 8 theorems belong to the basic theorems

Basic Theorem 1

Angle at the Centre

The angle subtended at the centre of a circle is twice the angle subtended at any point on the circumference, from the same arc.

If two chords from the same arc meet at the centre and at the rim, the central angle is the larger one. This links inscribed angles to arc geometry and is a foundation for many circle proofs.

Basic Theorem 2

Angles in a Semicircle

Any angle inscribed in a semicircle is a right angle (90°).

The diameter is the chord across the flat side of the semicircle. This is the special case of the angle-at-centre rule when the central angle is 180°.

Basic Theorem 3

Cyclic Quadrilateral

Opposite angles of a cyclic quadrilateral sum to 180°.

A cyclic quadrilateral has all four vertices on one circle. Opposite angles are supplementary, which often unlocks angle-chasing in harder diagrams.

! Converse idea: If one pair of opposite angles adds to 180°, the four points may be concyclic.
Basic Theorem 4

Radius to a Tangent

A radius drawn to the point of contact is perpendicular to the tangent: the angle between them is 90°.

A tangent touches the circle at exactly one point. The radius to that touching point meets the tangent at a right angle, which is key in construction and coordinate problems.

! Pair with: Combine with equal tangent lengths from an external point; many questions use both facts together.
Basic Theorem 5

Angles in the Same Segment

Angles subtended by the same chord (or arc) at the circumference, on the same side of the chord, are equal.

Treat the chord as dividing the circle into two arcs. Any point on the same arc sees the same chord under the same angle; switching arc gives the supplementary angle pair.

! Label carefully: Same segment means same side of the chord; do not mix angles from opposite arcs.
Basic Theorem 6

Tangents from a Point to a Circle

From an external point, the two tangent segments to a circle have equal lengths.

The two tangents form a symmetric V-shape from the external point. Equal tangent lengths often simplify perimeter and length equations.

! Construction: Mark the two right angles where radii meet tangents; then combine with Pythagoras when lengths are given.
Basic Theorem 7

Tangents from a Point (II)

The line from the external point to the centre bisects the angle between the two tangents, and bisects the angle between the two radii to the contact points.

Symmetry about the line through the external point and centre creates matched angles and distances. This is useful in loci and structured geometry proofs.

Basic Theorem 8

Alternate Segment Theorem

The angle between a tangent and a chord through the contact point equals the angle in the alternate segment, subtended by the chord in the opposite arc.

This links tangent direction to an inscribed angle on the opposite arc. It appears frequently in tangent-chord angle-chasing questions.

! Which segment? The alternate segment is the arc opposite the tangent-chord angle, not the arc containing that angle.

Advanced Theorem 4

Perpendicular Diameter Theorem

A diameter perpendicular to a chord bisects the chord and also bisects the two arcs subtended by that chord.

If a diameter meets chord AB at a right angle at point M, then AM = MB. By symmetry about that diameter, the minor arc AB is split into two equal arcs, and the corresponding major arc is also split into two equal arcs.

! Note: In proofs, mark the 90° first, then show the midpoint on the chord, and finally state equal arcs. This order is usually the clearest and earns method marks reliably.
Advanced Theorem 1

Secant Theorem

When two secant lines are drawn from a point outside a circle, the product of the distances from that point to the two intersection points on one secant equals the same product on the other secant.

Each secant meets the circle twice; multiply the two segments from the external point along that line. Those two products match—this is the “power of a point” for secants and often appears in length problems.

! Note: Write the four segments in order from the external point along each line; check you are not pairing the wrong chords before multiplying.
Advanced Theorem 2

Intersecting Chords Theorem

For two chords intersecting inside a circle, the products of the lengths of the two segments on each chord are equal.

If chords AB and CD meet at point P inside the circle, then AP * PB = CP * PD. This comes from equal angles in similar triangles formed by the intersecting chords, and it is a standard way to find unknown segment lengths.

Advanced Theorem 3

Tangent–Secant Theorem

From a point outside a circle, if a tangent and a secant are drawn, the tangent length is the mean proportional of the two secant segments from that point to the circle intersections.

Let P be the external point, PT the tangent, and the secant meet the circle at A and B (A nearer to P). Then PT^2 = PA * PB. So the tangent length is the geometric mean of the two secant lengths, which is a standard setup for length-solving questions.

Further Advanced Theorem 1

Point of Tangency 1

If ∠MNT + ∠PQT = ∠MTP and T lies on both circles, then T is the common point of tangency of the two circles.

The angle condition links the two circle configurations and forces the contact at T to be tangential rather than crossing. Since T is on both circles and the angle sum matches ∠MTP, the shared tangent direction at T is consistent for both circles, so T is their common tangency point.

Further Advanced Theorem 2

Point of Tangency 2

If T is a common tangency point of circles O₁ and O₂, then ∠ATB = ∠CTD; conversely, if ∠ATB = ∠CTD, then T is a common tangency point.

This is an equivalence theorem: the common-tangent condition at T and the angle equality condition determine each other. In one direction, tangency gives equal angle relations around T; in the reverse direction, the given angle equality forces the local geometry at T to match the tangency configuration of circles O₁ and O₂.

! Note: For “if and only if” questions, present both directions separately: first prove tangent => angle equality, then angle equality => tangent. Writing each implication clearly usually gains full method marks.
Further Advanced Theorem 3

Zhangjiao Theorem

∠BAC=α，∠DAC=β，则有：ABsinβ+ADsinα=ACsin(α+β)。

This identity can be proved using the Cyclic Quadrilateral theorem together with the Sine Rule.

! Note: When applying the formula, define α and β from the exact diagram first, then substitute lengths and angles in one consistent unit mode. Keep angle symbols on your working to avoid swapping α and β.
Further Advanced Theorem 4

Reim Theorem

AB is the common chord of two circles. MN is a chord of circle R. MA meets circle S again at P, and NB meets circle S again at Q. Then MN // PQ.

Proof: ∠NMA = ∠ABQ = 180° - ∠APQ, hence MN // PQ.

Further Advanced Theorem 5

Simson Theorem

From any point on the circumcircle of a triangle (other than the triangle’s vertices), draw perpendiculars to the three sides or their extensions; the three feet are collinear. This line is called the Simson line.

Proof: ∠PAC = ∠PBC, and ∠PAB = 180° - ∠PCA = ∠PBA, so ∠PAC + ∠PAB = 180°, hence the corresponding points are collinear.

! Note: In proofs, mark each right angle at the feet first, then convert to equal/supplementary angle relations around point P. A clean angle-chasing chain usually gives the fastest route to collinearity.
